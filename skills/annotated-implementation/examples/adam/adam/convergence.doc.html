Convergence of Adam

The convergence analysis in Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization (Section 4) proves that Adam's average regret goes to zero on convex problems. This file explains the setting, states the result, and sketches the proof. It also explains why the proof is wrong: Reddi et al. (2018) found a simple convex problem on which Adam converges to the worst possible point. The code implements that problem.

Online convex optimization

The analysis uses the online learning framework of Zinkevich (2003). At every step $t$ the optimizer commits to parameters $\theta_t$, and only then is it shown a convex cost function $f_t$ and pays $f_t(\theta_t)$. The sequence $f_1, f_2, \dots, f_T$ is arbitrary and unknown in advance. Minibatch training is the special case where each $f_t$ is the loss on a random minibatch.

The optimizer is measured by its regret: how much more it paid in total than the single best fixed parameter chosen with hindsight,

$$R(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \big[ f_t(\theta_t) - f_t(\theta^*) \big] \qquad \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(\theta)$$

Regret grows with $T$, and the question is how fast. If $R(T)$ grows more slowly than $T$, the average regret $R(T) / T$ goes to zero: on average the optimizer does as well as $\theta^*$. The best possible rate for general convex problems is $O(\sqrt{T})$.

The theorem

Write $g_t = \nabla f_t(\theta_t)$, $g_{t,i}$ for its $i$-th coordinate, and $g_{1:T,i} = [g_{1,i}, g_{2,i}, \dots, g_{T,i}]$ for the history of coordinate $i$. Let $\gamma = \beta_1^2 / \sqrt{\beta_2}$.

Theorem (Theorem 4.1 in the paper). Assume:

  • bounded gradients, $\lVert \nabla f_t(\theta) \rVert_2 \le G$ and $\lVert \nabla f_t(\theta) \rVert_\infty \le G_\infty$;
  • the iterates stay in a bounded region, $\lVert \theta_n - \theta_m \rVert_2 \le D$ and $\lVert \theta_n - \theta_m \rVert_\infty \le D_\infty$;
  • $\gamma \lt 1$;
  • the step size decays as $\alpha_t = \alpha / \sqrt{t}$, and the first-moment decay rate decays as $\beta_{1,t} = \beta_1 \lambda^{t-1}$ with $\lambda \in (0, 1)$.

Then for all $T \ge 1$,

\begin{align*} R(T) \le & \frac{D^2}{2 \alpha (1 - \beta_1)} \sum_{i=1}^{d} \sqrt{T \hat{v}_{T,i}} \\ & + \frac{\alpha (1 + \beta_1) G_\infty}{(1 - \beta_1) \sqrt{1 - \beta_2} (1 - \gamma)^2} \sum_{i=1}^{d} \lVert g_{1:T,i} \rVert_2 \\ & + \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{D_\infty^2 G_\infty \sqrt{1 - \beta_2}}{2 \alpha (1 - \beta_1) (1 - \lambda)^2} \end{align*}

Corollary (Corollary 4.2). Since $\lVert g_{1:T,i} \rVert_2 \le G_\infty \sqrt{T}$ and $\sqrt{T \hat{v}_{T,i}} \le G_\infty \sqrt{T}$, every term is $O(\sqrt{T})$, so $R(T) / T = O(1 / \sqrt{T})$ and the average regret goes to zero.

The bound depends on the gradients through $\lVert g_{1:T,i} \rVert_2$, coordinate by coordinate. When gradients are sparse these norms are much smaller than their worst case $G_\infty \sqrt{T}$. That is where adaptive methods like Adam and AdaGrad beat plain SGD.

The theorem is about a modified Adam. The algorithm as published, the implementation here, and every library implementation use a constant $\alpha$ and $\beta_1$. The theorem needs $\alpha_t = \alpha / \sqrt{t}$ and a $\beta_{1,t}$ that decays to zero.

Proof sketch

The paper gives the full proof in its appendix (Appendix 10.1). Its structure is as follows.

Proof sketch

1. Linearize. A convex function lies above its tangent planes (Lemma 10.2), so each term of the regret is bounded by a linear one:

$$f_t(\theta_t) - f_t(\theta^*) \le g_t^\top (\theta_t - \theta^*) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} g_{t,i} (\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)$$

2. Use the update rule. For one coordinate, subtract $\theta^*_i$ from both sides of the update and square. Solving for $g_{t,i}(\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)$ and applying Young's inequality $ab \le a^2 / 2 + b^2 / 2$ to the momentum cross term gives three kinds of terms:

  • a distance term $\frac{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}}}{2 \alpha_t (1 - \beta_1)} \big( (\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)^2 - (\theta_{t+1,i} - \theta^*_i)^2 \big)$,
  • a step-size term proportional to $\alpha_t \, \hat{m}_{t,i}^2 / \sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}}$,
  • a momentum term weighted by $\beta_{1,t}$.

3. Telescope the distance term. Summed over $t$, the differences of squared distances collapse, leaving terms of the form

$$\Big( \frac{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}}}{\alpha_t} - \frac{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t-1,i}}}{\alpha_{t-1}} \Big) (\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)^2$$

The proof bounds each $(\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)^2$ by $D_\infty^2$, and the sum collapses to $D_\infty^2 \sqrt{\hat{v}_{T,i}} / \alpha_T$. That gives the first term of the bound.

4. Bound the step-size term. A lemma (Lemma 10.4) expands $\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t$ as sums over past gradients and bounds $\sum_t \hat{m}_{t,i}^2 / \sqrt{t \hat{v}_{t,i}}$ using the arithmetic-geometric series in $\gamma$. This is where $\gamma \lt 1$ is needed. A second lemma (Lemma 10.3), proved by induction on $T$, then turns $\sum_t \sqrt{g_{t,i}^2 / t}$ into $2 G_\infty \lVert g_{1:T,i} \rVert_2$. That gives the second term.

5. Bound the momentum term. Because $\beta_{1,t} = \beta_1 \lambda^{t-1}$, the momentum terms form a geometric series in $\lambda$, and their sum is bounded by the third term.

The extracted text of the first lemma (Lemma 10.4) states a bound with $1 / (1 - \gamma)$ and no $G_\infty$, but its proof ends with $G_\infty / (1 - \gamma)^2$. The theorem uses the second form.

Where the proof fails

Step 3 only works if every coefficient $\frac{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t,i}}}{\alpha_t} - \frac{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t-1,i}}}{\alpha_{t-1}}$ is non-negative. Only then can each $(\theta_{t,i} - \theta^*_i)^2$ be replaced by its upper bound $D_\infty^2$. That requires $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} / \alpha_t$ to never decrease. For SGD and AdaGrad it doesn't. For Adam it can: $\hat{v}_t$ is an exponential moving average, so it drops whenever recent gradients are smaller than old ones.

The counterexample

Reddi et al. (2018) turn this into a counterexample. On $\mathcal{X} = [-1, 1]$, with $C \gt 2$, let

$$f_t(x) = \begin{cases} C x & t \bmod 3 = 1 \\ -x & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Every three steps the costs add up to $(C - 2) x$, so the best point is $x = -1$. Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ and $\beta_2 = 1 / (1 + C^2)$ moves towards $x = +1$ instead. The large gradient $C$ that carries the information arrives once every three steps. Dividing by $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ shrinks it to a step about the same size as each $-1$ step, and $\hat{v}_t$ decays before the next one arrives. Two steps towards $+1$ outweigh one step towards $-1$, and the regret grows linearly in $T$.

The gradient of $f_t$ at any $x$: $C$ when $t \bmod 3 = 1$, and $-1$ otherwise.

Result

We ran the problem for $T = 6000$ steps with $\alpha = 0.5$, for $C = 3$ and $C = 10$. Adam moves to $x = +1$, the worst point, in less than 50 steps and stays there. At $x = +1$ the optimizer pays $2 (C - 2)$ more than at $x = -1$ in every three steps, so its average regret goes to $2 (C - 2) / 3$, not to zero. At $T = 6000$ it is $0.671$ for $C = 3$ and $5.330$ for $C = 10$. The conclusion of the theorem does not hold for this problem.

Reddi et al. fix the proof with AMSGrad, which divides by the running maximum $\max(\hat{v}_{t-1}, v_t)$ instead of $\hat{v}_t$. That makes $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} / \alpha_t$ non-decreasing by construction. On the same problem, PyTorch's torch.optim.Adam(amsgrad=True) ends below $x = -0.9$, and its average regret falls to about $0.01$. In practice Adam rarely runs into this, but the convergence guarantee in the paper does not cover it.
